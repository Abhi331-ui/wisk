# Five mystery shorts: scripts v1 (2026-11-16)
Format: cartoon-3d-short skill. ~3.7 words/s, 55-70 s, one steady narrator, no captions.
Fact status: ✅ verified true story · ⚠️ composite / viral tale (no single documented case). The jokes and explanations are real facts in every script.

## 1. The Tree That Grew Spaghetti ✅
Facts: BBC *Panorama*, 1 April 1957, ~3 min report narrated by the respected broadcaster Richard Dimbleby (not named in the VO).
Swiss family "harvesting" spaghetti from trees in Ticino after a mild winter, with "the virtual disappearance of the spaghetti weevil".
It explained that the strands are all the same length thanks to "many years of patient endeavour by past breeders". Millions watched,
and hundreds phoned in. Some asked where to buy a tree. The BBC replied: "place a sprig of spaghetti in a tin of tomato sauce and hope
for the best." Some viewers complained that a news show had played a joke. In 1950s Britain spaghetti was mostly known as a tinned food.
(Wikipedia "Spaghetti-tree hoax"; BBC On This Day 1 April 1957)

In 1957, the BBC convinced half of Britain that spaghetti grows on trees.
It's April the first, and millions of families are watching the most serious news show on television.
The calm, trusted narrator takes them to a sunny Swiss village after a very mild winter.
This year, the spaghetti trees are having a bumper crop.
A smiling family plucks long strands of pasta off the branches and lays them out in the sun to dry.
He even explains that the dreaded spaghetti weevil has almost disappeared.
And why is every strand exactly the same length? Generations of patient breeding, of course.
Then the phones at the BBC start ringing, and they don't stop.
Hundreds of viewers want to know one thing: where can I buy a spaghetti tree?
Luckily, it was an April Fools' joke.
Unluckily, almost nobody in Britain knew what spaghetti was made of.
You see, in 1957 most British people had only ever seen spaghetti in one place: inside a tin, swimming in tomato sauce.
Think about it. If the most boring, trustworthy man you know told you pasta grows on trees, you'd probably believe him too.
And this was the most trustworthy voice on British television.
Some viewers were furious that the news had lied to them. Others just wanted their tree.
So the BBC gave them official gardening advice: place a sprig of spaghetti in a tin of tomato sauce, and hope for the best.

## 2. The Sandwich Thief ⚠️
Story status: this is a well-known kind of viral office tale. I found no single documented case of a raccoon raiding an office fridge through
the ceiling. Raccoons do regularly get into buildings through ceilings and attics, and some fall through ceiling tiles.
Real facts used: raccoons' front paws are extremely sensitive, and about two-thirds of the touch-processing area of their cortex handles
touch from the paws. In H. B. Davis's 1907 lock study, raccoons opened 11 of 13 complex locks.
(Wikipedia "Raccoon": Senses, Intelligence)
Option: keep it as a "true-style" story, or say "The story goes..." in the hook.

An entire office spent weeks hunting a sandwich thief who only ever ate the middle.
Every morning, one employee puts a fancy homemade sandwich in the breakroom fridge.
Every lunchtime, the bag is still there, the box is still there, and the lid is even closed.
But inside, there's nothing left except four perfect crusts.
Management sends a stern email about workplace theft. Then a sterner one.
People start glaring at each other over the coffee machine.
Gary from accounts has crumbs on his desk. Gary is now the main suspect.
Finally, they install a security camera.
At three in the morning, a ceiling tile slides open, and a raccoon climbs down.
It pulls open the fridge, pops the lid, eats the filling, closes the box, and disappears back into the ceiling.
Luckily, Gary was innocent.
Unluckily, the thief had better table manners than Gary.
You see, a raccoon's paws work almost like little hands, and a huge part of its brain is devoted just to touch.
Over a hundred years ago, a scientist gave raccoons thirteen complicated locks, and they cracked eleven of them.
Ever opened a jar in the dark without looking? That's how a raccoon sees the world, through its fingers.
To an animal like that, a fridge door held shut by a magnet is basically an invitation.
The office fixed the ceiling, and the emails finally stopped.
But nobody ever looked at Gary the same way again.

## 3. The Bloop ✅
Facts: summer 1997, NOAA's underwater microphones (hydrophones from a US Navy listening network built to track submarines) recorded an
ultra-low-frequency sound from the remote South Pacific (~50°S 100°W). It was picked up by sensors more than 5,000 km (3,000+ miles) apart,
and was far louder than any known animal. Lovecraft fans noted it came from roughly the same remote region as his fictional sunken
city R'lyeh. In 2012 NOAA matched it to icequakes: icebergs cracking and calving off Antarctic glaciers.
Sound gets trapped in the deep-ocean SOFAR channel and travels thousands of km. The public NOAA clip is sped up 16x because the real
sound is too low to hear well. (Wikipedia "Bloop"; NOAA PMEL Acoustics)

In 1997, the ocean made a sound so loud that people thought scientists had found a sea monster.
America keeps secret underwater microphones scattered across the Pacific, built to listen for enemy submarines.
One summer, they pick up something new: a deep, rising roar from the far South Pacific.
It's heard by microphones more than three thousand miles apart.
It's louder than any animal known to science, louder than a blue whale.
Scientists give it a name: the Bloop.
The internet does not take it calmly.
Worse, it came from the same lonely corner of the ocean where a famous horror writer hid the sunken city of his giant tentacled monster.
Giant squid. A living megalodon. Cthulhu himself.
Then, years later, scientists finally hear the exact same sound again, coming from near Antarctica.
Luckily, the monster wasn't real.
Unluckily, the truth was ice.
You see, when a giant iceberg cracks off a glacier, it's called an icequake, and it's incredibly loud.
Deep in the ocean, there's a layer of water that traps sound and carries it for thousands of miles.
It's like whispering into one end of a very long pipe. The sound can't spread out, so it arrives on the other side almost as loud.
Even the spooky recording you can hear online is sped up sixteen times, because the real thing is too low for human ears.
So the most terrifying monster ever heard in the ocean turned out to be the same thing floating in your drink.

## 4. The Haunted Roomba ⚠️
Story status: composite viral story. Cats riding robot vacuums are very real and very viral (the famous "shark cat" videos from 2012),
but I found no single documented "3 a.m. haunting" case.
Real facts used: robot vacuums have a large CLEAN button on top that a paw can press. Classic Roombas find walls by bumping into them
and turning. Cats are crepuscular and naturally active in the dark. (Wikipedia "Roomba"; "Cat": Behavior)

A family was convinced their house was haunted, because their vacuum kept cleaning at three in the morning.
Every night, while everyone sleeps, the robot vacuum switches itself on.
It doesn't clean. It slams into the same wall, over and over.
By morning, there are zigzag scuffs across the floor, and everything in one corner is knocked over.
Like it's trying to escape something only it can see.
They check the schedule. Nothing. They reset it. It happens again.
So one night, they turn off the lights, hide on the stairs, and wait.
Three a.m. The vacuum beeps.
And there, sitting on top of it like a tiny furry captain, is the cat.
Luckily, the house wasn't haunted.
Unluckily, the ghost still expected breakfast at six.
You see, robot vacuums have a big button right on top, and one paw is all it takes to start a cleaning run.
Cats are naturally most active in the dark, when they'd normally be out hunting.
And most of these robots don't really see walls. They find them by bumping into them and turning away.
It's like walking through your house at night with your arms out. Bump, turn. Bump, turn.
With a cat leaning on one side, every turn bends the same way, straight into the same corner.
So the family did what any reasonable people would do.
They didn't get rid of the cat. They moved the furniture.

## 5. The Croissant in the Tree ✅
Facts: Kraków, Poland, 2021. A woman called KTOZ (the Kraków Animal Welfare Society) about an unknown creature that had sat in a tree outside
her window for two days. Neighbours were frightened. The inspector found a croissant wedged in the branches. KTOZ shared the story online
good-naturedly and said she was right to call. (BBC News / Notes from Poland, 2021; KTOZ Facebook post)
Real facts used: pareidolia, and the brain's bias toward seeing animals and faces in vague shapes (the "better a false alarm" survival bias).

In 2021, animal rescuers in Poland were called out to catch a dangerous creature that turned out to be breakfast.
It's Kraków, and a woman stares out her window at something brown and wrinkled sitting in a tree.
It has no head, it has no legs, and it hasn't moved for two whole days.
The neighbors are keeping their windows shut, just in case it flies inside.
So she calls the city's animal welfare society.
This could be an escaped reptile, an exotic bird, maybe something venomous.
An inspector shows up ready for anything.
Gloves on. Ladder up. Slowly, carefully, he reaches into the branches...
and pulls out a croissant.
Luckily, nobody got bitten.
Unluckily, it had been out there so long that nobody wanted to bite it either.
You see, your brain is a pattern-finding machine, and when it isn't sure what it's looking at, it guesses animal first.
For our ancestors, mistaking a stick for a snake was harmless. Mistaking a snake for a stick was not.
Ever woken up at night and seen someone standing in your room, and it was just your coat on a chair?
Same thing, but with pastry.
Somebody had simply tossed it out of a window.
And the rescuers weren't even annoyed. They said she did exactly the right thing by calling.
So the wildest animal in Kraków that week was safely captured, and the only victim was the croissant.
